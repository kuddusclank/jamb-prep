# JAMB Literature in English — Comprehensive Study Note for Candidates (Jambites)

**Subject Code:** `LIT`  
**Total Questions in UTME:** 40 Questions  
**Time Target:** 25 – 27 Minutes  
**Maximum Scaled Score:** 100 Marks  
**Authoritative Reference:** Official JAMB IBASS Syllabus for Literature in English  

---

## Overview & How JAMB Tests Literature in English

In the UTME, Literature in English is tested via **40 multiple-choice questions** grouped into four major segments:
1. **General Literary Principles, Terms & Unseen Appreciation (~10 Questions):** Genres, figures of speech, dramatic and poetic conventions, metric forms, rhyming patterns, and critical analysis of unseen short prose excerpts and poems.
2. **Prescribed African and Non-African Prose (~10 Questions):** Plot developments, character traits and motivations, themes, narrative techniques, and socio-historical settings in prescribed novels.
3. **Prescribed African and Non-African Drama (~10 Questions):** Dramatic conflict, acts, scenes, character foils, stagecraft, dramatic irony, soliloquies, and thematic messages.
4. **Prescribed African and Non-African Poetry (~10 Questions):** Stanza-by-stanza structural analysis, diction, tone, mood, poetic imagery, symbolic motifs, and themes in prescribed poems.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Essential Preparation Advice for JAMB Candidates:**
> - JAMB tests the **exact details of prescribed texts**, not generic summaries. Candidates must know specific character relationships, quotes, names of minor characters, key turning points, and symbolic objects.
> - For Unseen Appreciation questions, focus strictly on identifying the speaker's **tone** (attitude), the resulting **mood** (atmosphere created in the reader), and the specific literary device deployed in cited lines.

# SECTION 1: LITERARY PRINCIPLES, CONVENTIONS & FIGURATIVE DEVICES

## 1. The Three Major Literary Genres
- **Drama:** A literary composition written in dialogue form designed to be performed by actors on a stage before an audience.
  - *Tragedy:* Depicts the downfall of a noble protagonist (tragic hero) caused by a tragic flaw (**hamartia**), overweening pride (**hubris**), or fate, eliciting pity and fear (**catharsis**) in the audience.
  - *Comedy:* Light-hearted, humorous play designed to amuse and entertain, ending happily (often with marriage or reconciliation).
  - *Tragicomedy:* Blends both tragic and comic elements, containing potential catastrophe that resolves happily.
  - *Melodrama:* Highly sensational drama with exaggerated emotions, stereotypical characters, and simplistic moral conflict.
  - *Farce:* Low comedy characterized by absurd situations, physical slapstick, and crude humor.
- **Poetry:** Intense, rhythmic expression of thoughts, feelings, and experiences through imaginative language, imagery, meter, and sound devices.
- **Prose:** Continuous, straightforward narrative written in ordinary grammatical sentences and paragraphs without regular metric pattern. Divided into **Fiction** (novels, novellas, short stories) and **Non-Fiction** (biographies, autobiographies, memoirs, essays).

---

## 2. Essential Dramatic Terms
- **Soliloquy:** A dramatic device where an actor speaks their innermost private thoughts aloud alone on stage, revealing their true character, fears, or plans directly to the audience.
- **Aside:** A brief remark spoken by a character directly to the audience (or to one other character) that is conventionally understood to be **unheard by other characters** on stage.
- **Dramatic Irony:** Occurs when the audience or reader possesses vital knowledge that a character on stage is completely ignorant of, creating tension, suspense, or foreboding.
- **Foreshadowing:** Subtle clues or hints given early in a plot anticipating future outcomes or tragic disasters.
- **Deus ex Machina:** An unexpected, artificial, or improbable character, device, or event introduced suddenly to resolve a seemingly insoluble plot entanglement.
- **Chorus:** A group of performers in classical drama who comment on the action, articulate moral themes, and provide background exposition without actively driving the plot.
- **Character Foil:** A character whose traits sharply contrast with another character (usually the protagonist) to emphasize the other's distinct qualities (e.g., Lakunle as foil to Baroka in *The Lion and the Jewel*).

---

## 3. Essential Poetic Terms & Metric Forms
- **Stanzaic Forms:**
  - Couplet ($2$ lines, usually rhyming $aa$)
  - Tercet ($3$ lines)
  - Quatrain ($4$ lines, most common)
  - Sestet ($6$ lines)
  - Octave ($8$ lines)
- **The Sonnet:** A 14-line lyrical poem written in iambic pentameter:
  - *Petrarchan / Italian Sonnet:* Divided into an **Octave** (rhyme scheme $abbaabba$) stating a problem, and a **Sestet** ($cdecde$ or $cdcdcd$) resolving it. The sharp shift in thought between octave and sestet is called the **Volta** (turn).
  - *Shakespearean / English Sonnet:* Divided into **Three Quatrains** ($abab\ cdcd\ efef$) exploring different facets of a theme, concluding with a rhyming **Heroic Couplet** ($gg$) delivering a definitive conclusion or epigrammatic punchline.
- **Types of Poems:**
  - *Elegy:* A somber, meditative poem lamenting the death of a specific person or mourning the transience of life.
  - *Dirge:* A short, mournful funeral song of grief sung at a burial.
  - *Ode:* A formal, dignified lyric poem addressed to celebrate or praise a person, object, or abstract concept (e.g., Keats' "Ode on a Grecian Urn").
  - *Ballad:* A narrative folk poem telling a tragic or romantic story in regular quatrains with a repeated refrain ($abcb$).
  - *Epic:* A lengthy, grand narrative poem celebrating heroic deeds, legendary figures, and national myths (e.g., Homer's *Iliad*).
  - *Blank Verse:* Unrhymed poetry written in strict **iambic pentameter** (standard medium of Shakespeare's plays).
  - *Free Verse:* Poetry free from regular meter, rhyme, or fixed stanzaic patterns; rhythm depends on cadence and line breaks.

## 4. Comprehensive Glossary of Figures of Speech
- **Comparison & Association:**
  - *Simile:* Explicit comparison using "as" or "like" (e.g., "His words were sharp like a razor").
  - *Metaphor:* Direct, implicit comparison stating one thing is another without using "like" or "as" (e.g., "The Lord is my shepherd", "Time is a thief").
  - *Personification:* Attributing human traits, feelings, or actions to inanimate objects or abstractions (e.g., "The angry sea swallowed the ship").
  - *Apostrophe:* Directly addressing an absent person, dead figure, or personified abstract quality as if present and listening (e.g., "O Death, where is thy sting?", "Milton! Thou shouldst be living at this hour").
  - *Synecdoche:* Using a part of something to represent the whole, or the whole to represent a part (e.g., "All hands on deck" where *hands* represent sailors; "Nigeria won the match" where *Nigeria* represents the national football team).
  - *Metonymy:* Substituting the name of an object or concept with something closely associated with it (e.g., "The pen is mightier than the sword", "The Crown announced new measures").
- **Contrast, Irony & Contradiction:**
  - *Oxymoron:* Placing two opposite or contradictory words immediately side by side (e.g., "cruel kindness", "deafening silence", "bitter sweet", "open secret").
  - *Paradox:* A statement that appears self-contradictory or absurd on the surface, but reveals a profound truth upon deeper reflection (e.g., "The child is father of the man", "More haste, less speed", "Cowards die many times before their deaths").
  - *Antithesis:* Setting contrasting ideas in balanced, parallel grammatical structures (e.g., "To err is human, to forgive divine", "Speech is silver, but silence is golden").
  - *Irony:* A contrast between appearance and reality:
    - *Verbal Irony:* Stating the opposite of what is actually meant (sarcasm when intended to mock).
    - *Situational Irony:* An outcome that turns out completely contrary to what was logically expected.
- **Sound Devices:**
  - *Alliteration:* Repetition of initial consonant sounds in neighboring words (e.g., "The **f**air **b**reeze **b**lew, the white **f**oam **f**lew").
  - *Assonance:* Repetition of identical or similar vowel sounds within nearby words (e.g., "Cl**e**an sh**ee**t of p**a**per").
  - *Onomatopoeia:* A word whose pronunciation phonetically imitates the natural sound it describes (e.g., *buzz, hiss, clatter, splash, rattle*).
- **Exaggeration & Understatement:**
  - *Hyperbole:* Deliberate and extreme exaggeration for emphasis or comic effect (e.g., "I haven't seen you in a million years", "She cried an ocean of tears").
  - *Litotes:* Deliberate understatement by negating the opposite (e.g., "He is no amateur" = he is a master; "That was no mean feat" = it was a magnificent achievement).
  - *Euphemism:* Substituting a mild, delicate, or indirect expression for an unpleasant, harsh, or taboo reality (e.g., "passed away" for died; "economically disadvantaged" for poor; "between jobs" for unemployed).

# SECTION 2: PRESCRIBED AFRICAN AND NON-AFRICAN PROSE

## 5. African Prose Textual Analysis
### *Second Class Citizen* — Buchi Emecheta
- **Plot Summary:** Follows the journey of **Adah Ofili**, an ambitious Igbo girl in colonial and post-colonial Nigeria who fights against gender bias to attain secondary education, secures an elite library job, and marries **Francis Obi**. Francis travels to the UK to study accountancy; Adah subsequently joins him in London with their children. In England, Adah confronts fierce institutional racism, squalid living conditions, poverty, and Francis's descent into religious fanaticism, misogyny, and domestic violence. Despite Francis burning her first manuscript (*The Bride Price*), Adah demonstrates resilient self-determination, divorces him, and secures an independent future for her children as a successful writer.
- **Core Characters:**
  - *Adah Ofili:* Courageous, intellectual protagonist; symbol of female emancipation and perseverance.
  - *Francis Obi:* Insecure, abusive, patriarchal husband; fails his exams and relies parasitically on Adah's income while physically assaulting her.
  - *Pa Noble:* Elderly Nigerian immigrant landlord in London who achieved fame by swallowing hot worms for white entertainment; provides Adah accommodation when white landlords reject them.
  - *Mr. and Mrs. Babalola:* Fellow Nigerian tenants in London illustrating the realities of immigrant survival.
  - *Bill:* Adah's supportive white Canadian library colleague who introduces her to African-American literature (James Baldwin) and encourages her writing.
- **Major Themes:** Gender oppression and patriarchy, racial discrimination in 1960s Britain, the harsh immigrant experience, the transformative power of female education, and marital infidelity and abuse.

### *Unexpected Joy at Dawn* — Alex Agyei-Agyiri
- **Plot Summary:** Explores the tragic repercussions of political xenophobia and mass deportations between Ghana and Nigeria during the 1980s. Protagonist **Nii Tackie**, a Ghanaian bank clerk of Nigerian parentage living in Accra, suffers persecution under Ghana's Aliens Compliance Order (1969). His sister, **Mama Orojo**, resides in Nigeria. When Ghana's economy collapses, Nii embarks on an illegal migratory journey to Nigeria in search of his family roots, just as Nigeria prepares to expel millions of illegal Ghanaian immigrants ("Ghana Must Go", 1983). The siblings navigate immigration barriers, corruption, and social upheaval before achieving unexpected reunion and hope.
- **Core Characters & Themes:** Nii Tackie, Mama Orojo, Masa, Joe, Massa. Themes: Xenophobia and state-sponsored expulsions, border artificiality, cross-border economic migration, religious exploitation, and the enduring strength of blood ties.

## 6. Non-African Prose Textual Analysis
### *Wuthering Heights* — Emily Brontë
- **Plot Summary:** Set on the bleak Yorkshire moors, the novel traces the destructive, all-consuming passion between **Heathcliff**, an orphaned gypsy boy adopted by Mr. Earnshaw, and Earnshaw's daughter, **Catherine Earnshaw**. Hindley Earnshaw violently abuses Heathcliff after their father's death. Catherine, though loving Heathcliff passionately ("I *am* Heathcliff"), chooses to marry the refined, wealthy **Edgar Linton** of Thrushcross Grange for social status. Heathcliff disappears, returns as a wealthy, vindictive gentleman, and exacts ruthless vengeance across two generations (Hindley, Catherine, Linton Heathcliff, Cathy Linton, and Hareton) before dying haunted by Catherine's ghost.
- **Core Characters & Setting:** Heathcliff, Catherine Earnshaw, Edgar Linton, Hindley, Nelly Dean (housekeeper/chief narrator), Mr. Lockwood (outsider framing narrator). Settings: *Wuthering Heights* (wild, stormy, passionate, primitive) vs. *Thrushcross Grange* (civilized, calm, wealthy, domestic).
- **Major Themes:** Destructive love vs. societal ambition, revenge and generational trauma, social class and Victorian snobbery, nature vs. civilization, and the supernatural.

### *Invisible Man* — Ralph Ellison
- **Plot & Thematic Core:** An unnamed African-American narrator journeys through 1930s America (Deep South to Harlem), encountering institutional racism, ideological hypocrisy in the communist "Brotherhood", and social marginalization. He realizes that white society refuses to see him as an individual human being ("I am an invisible man... simply because people refuse to see me").

# SECTION 3: PRESCRIBED AFRICAN AND NON-AFRICAN DRAMA

## 7. African Drama Textual Analysis
### *The Lion and the Jewel* — Wole Soyinka
- **Plot Summary:** A light-hearted yet biting satirical comedy set in the Yoruba village of **Ilujinle**. The plot revolves around the competition between two men for the hand of **Sidi**, the exquisite village belle whose ego has skyrocketed after her photographs are published in a glamorous glossy magazine.
  - **Lakunle:** The young, idealistic village schoolteacher who champions uncritical European "civilization", dressing in oversized European suits and rejecting the payment of traditional bride price as "barbaric".
  - **Baroka (The Bale):** The crafty, 62-year-old traditional ruler (the "Lion") who embodies native wisdom, virility, and traditional hegemony.
  - Hearing of Sidi's pride, Baroka feigns that his manhood has failed (impotence). He entrusts this secret to his first wife, **Sadiku**, knowing she will gossip to Sidi.
  - Sidi visits the palace to mock the vanquished "Lion", but Baroka outwits her, seduces her, and deflowers her. Sidi ultimately chooses to marry the virile, authentic Baroka, vigorously rejecting Lakunle's empty Europeanized platitudes.
- **Key Characters:**
  - *Sidi (The Jewel):* Beautiful village girl whose vanity leads to her seduction.
  - *Baroka (The Lion):* Cunning traditional Bale who defends African customs through shrewd psychological warfare.
  - *Lakunle:* Comic foil; half-educated schoolteacher spouting dictionary jargon without genuine understanding.
  - *Sadiku:* Senior wife and matchmaker whose boastful indiscretion catalyzes the comic climax.
- **Major Themes:** Tradition vs. Modernity (the conflict between superficial Westernization and enduring African traditional institutions), vanity and deceit, and the politics of gender and power.

### *Women of Owu* — Femi Osofisan
- **Dramatic Core:** A poignant reinterpretation of Euripides' *The Trojan Women* in the context of 19th-century Yoruba warfare. Depicts the horrific fall, plunder, and destruction of the ancient fortified kingdom of **Owu** by the combined Allied forces of Ife, Ijebu, and Oyo mercenaries. Centers on the suffering, sorrow, and philosophical resilience of the captive royal women (**Erelu Afin**, Queen Mother; **Orisaye**, visionary priestess; **Adumaadan**). Themes: The cruelty and futility of war, the victimization of women in conflict, divine indifference, and post-war reconstruction.

## 8. Non-African Drama Textual Analysis
### *A Midsummer Night's Dream* — William Shakespeare
- **Plot Summary:** Composed of four intertwining plots set around Athens and an enchanted moonlit forest:
  1. The impending royal wedding of **Theseus**, Duke of Athens, and **Hippolyta**, Queen of the Amazons.
  2. The romantic quadrilateral among four young Athenians: **Hermia** loves **Lysander**, but her father Egeus demands she marry **Demetrius**, who is desperately loved by **Helena**. The lovers flee into the magical forest.
  3. The fairy kingdom feud between **Oberon** (King of Fairies) and **Titania** (Queen of Fairies) over an Indian changeling boy. Oberon orders his mischievous fairy servant, **Puck (Robin Goodfellow)**, to drop the juice of the magic flower "love-in-idleness" onto sleeping eyes.
  4. The "Rude Mechanicals" (Athenian working men led by **Nick Bottom** the weaver) rehearsing the comical play *Pyramus and Thisbe*.
  - Puck's blunders cause both Lysander and Demetrius to fall frantically in love with Helena, while Titania awakes to fall enamored with Bottom, whose head has been magically transformed into that of an ass/donkey. Order is eventually restored, Puck corrects the charms, Theseus sanctions the three weddings, and the mechanicals perform their hilarious play.
- **Key Lines & Devices:**
  - *"The course of true love never did run smooth"* (Lysander).
  - *"Lord, what fools these mortals be!"* (Puck).
  - Extensive use of dramatic irony, soliloquy, rhyming verse, and the "play-within-a-play" device.

# SECTION 4: PRESCRIBED AFRICAN AND NON-AFRICAN POETRY

## 9. Key Prescribed African Poems
- **"Piano and Drums" — Gabriel Okara:**
  - *Theme:* Cultural conflict and the post-colonial African identity crisis.
  - *Structure:* The rustic raw rhythm of the **African drum** (symbolizing ancestral heritage, vitality, warmth, and pastoral simplicity) contrasts sharply with the intricate, labyrinthine melody of the **Western piano** (symbolizing complex, cold, mechanized European technology). The poet-speaker ends in perplexity, wandering between two conflicting civilizations.
- **"The Leader and the Led" — Niyi Osundare:**
  - *Theme:* The search for balanced, ethical political leadership in Africa.
  - *Allegory:* Uses an animal fable where different beasts demand leadership based on flawed, extreme traits: the Lion (too ferocious), the Antelope (too timid), the Giraffe (eyes too far above the ground), the Elephant (too heavy/destructive), the Warthog (too ugly), the Rhinoceros (too riotous). Concludes that true leadership requires a blend of complementary virtues: *"A little bit of a lion / A little bit of a lamb"*.
- **"Black Woman" ("Femme Noire") — Léopold Sédar Senghor:**
  - *Theme:* Celebration of African beauty, womanhood, and culture (classic **Négritude** movement).
  - *Imagery:* Compares the black woman's skin to dark ripe fruit, savannah plains, and drum beats, elevating her to a cosmic archetype of Mother Africa.

## 10. Key Prescribed Non-African Poems
- **"The Pulley" — George Herbert:**
  - *Theme:* God's divine relationship with mankind; the source of human spiritual restlessness.
  - *Metaphysical Conceit:* God pours all blessings from a glass onto man (strength, beauty, wisdom, honor) but deliberately withholds **"Rest"** at the bottom of the glass. By keeping man rich in gifts yet constantly weary and unsatisfied, man's very restlessness will act as a "pulley" drawing him back to his Creator.
- **"Shall I Compare Thee to a Summer's Day?" (Sonnet 18) — William Shakespeare:**
  - *Theme:* The immortalizing power of poetry over mortality and time.
  - *Argument:* Summer is imperfect (too short, too hot, dimmed by clouds), but the beloved's eternal beauty will never fade because it is captured forever in the poet's timeless verse: *"So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, / So long lives this, and this gives life to thee."*

# SECTION 5: 10 GOLDEN STRATEGIES FOR JAMB LITERATURE IN ENGLISH

1. **Differentiate Tone from Mood:** **Tone** is the author's/speaker's personal attitude toward the subject matter (e.g., sarcastic, mournful, didactic). **Mood** is the emotional atmosphere evoked inside *you* the reader (e.g., somber, joyful, tense).
2. **Master the Two Ironies:** If a character says "What lovely weather!" during a catastrophic thunderstorm, it is **Verbal Irony**. If King Duncan trusts Macbeth as an honorable host while the audience knows Macbeth is planning his murder, it is **Dramatic Irony**.
3. **Know the Literary Movements:** *Négritude* is associated with Senghor (pride in African heritage); *Metaphysical poetry* is associated with John Donne and George Herbert (intellectual conceits, paradoxes).
4. **Identify the Volta in Sonnets:** In a Petrarchan sonnet, always look at line 9. Words like *"Yet", "But", "Now"* signal the pivotal shift (Volta) from the problem in the octave to the resolution in the sestet.
5. **Oxymoron vs. Paradox Trap:** An **oxymoron** is strictly two contradictory words side-by-side ("sweet sorrow", "living dead"). A **paradox** is an entire statement or complete idea that seems contradictory yet carries deep truth.
6. **Track the Narrator in Prose:** In *Second Class Citizen*, it is third-person limited focusing closely on Adah's consciousness. In *Wuthering Heights*, it is a multi-layered framing narrative (Lockwood hears the story from Nelly Dean).
7. **Baroka's Impotence Trick:** Remember that Baroka's impotence in *The Lion and the Jewel* was a deliberate hoax devised to test Sadiku's ability to keep secrets and trap the haughty Sidi!
8. **Shakespeare's Meter:** Shakespeare's high-born noble characters speak in **blank verse** (unrhymed iambic pentameter), while comic, lower-class working characters (like the mechanicals) speak in **colloquial prose**.
9. **Recognize Synecdoche vs Metonymy:** Synecdoche uses an actual physical **part of the thing** ("twenty head of cattle", "wheels" for car). Metonymy uses a completely separate object that is **symbolically associated** with it ("the White House" for the US President).
10. **Apostrophe Addresses the Absent:** When a speaker in a poem cries out to an abstraction or dead figure ("O Sun!", "O Death!"), the answer is **Apostrophe**, not merely personification.
